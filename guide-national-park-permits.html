National park permits and timed entry, decoded for 2026

By Marisol Trevino, Field editor Published 03/09/2026 Reviewed 03/09/2026 12 min read Category: National parks

N 38.5733 / W 109.5498 · SEC 01 · BODY

The most common way an American road trip goes wrong in 2026 is arriving at a park gate without the reservation you did not know existed. It is not one system. It is four, and they open on different schedules.

Four systems, not one

Everything sold through Recreation.gov looks the same on the website and behaves completely differently in practice:

  • Timed entry. A cheap reservation for a specific arrival window. Controls how many vehicles are inside at once. Seasonal.
  • Lotteries. A random draw for a specific trail or feature where demand massively exceeds capacity. You apply, you pay a small fee, you may not win.
  • Campgrounds. First-come or reservable sites, released on a rolling window, gone in seconds at the popular parks.
  • Wilderness permits. Required for overnight backcountry travel, sometimes for day use on specific routes. Often quota-controlled.

None of these replace the entrance fee or the annual pass. You can hold a timed entry reservation and still be turned away for not paying entry, and vice versa.

Timed entry: what it is and which parks use it

Timed entry is a free-or-cheap reservation, typically $2 to $3 in processing, that gives you a window in which to drive through the gate. Once you are in, you stay as long as you like. It exists because parking built in 1965 cannot hold 2026 visitation.

Two release patterns cover almost all of these. A block goes on sale several months ahead at 10:00 AM Mountain, and a smaller next-day block releases at 7:00 PM Mountain the evening before. The next-day block is how most people actually get in, and it is worth setting an alarm for.

Confirm the current rules on the park's own nps.gov page before you travel. These programs are reviewed annually and dates shift.

Lotteries: Angels Landing, Half Dome, The Wave

Lotteries exist where a single feature has a hard capacity limit. The three that dominate planning:

Angels Landing, Zion. The chained final section requires a permit. There is a seasonal lottery months ahead and a day-before lottery that closes mid-afternoon Mountain time. Applying costs $6 for up to six people, and winning costs $3 per person more. If you do not win, Scout Lookout is the same hike minus the last half mile and needs no permit at all. The view is very nearly identical.

Half Dome, Yosemite. A preseason lottery in March plus a daily lottery two days ahead during the season. The cables go up in late May and come down in October. Success rates in the daily lottery are meaningfully better midweek.

The Wave, Coyote Buttes North. Managed by the Bureau of Land Management rather than the Park Service. Sixty-four people a day. The advance lottery runs about four months ahead and the daily geofenced lottery requires you to be physically in the area. Odds in peak months sit in the low single digits.

The planning lesson is not to build a trip around a lottery. Build the trip, apply for the lottery, and treat winning as a bonus day rather than the reason you flew.

Put every release date in the plan

Add each lottery and release date as a note in the trip planner so it prints on your itinerary. Our Utah Mighty 5 plan has the Arches and Angels Landing timing built into day one and day four.

Campgrounds and the six-month window

Most national park campgrounds open a rolling six-month booking window at 10:00 AM Eastern on Recreation.gov. At popular parks, the good sites are gone inside sixty seconds. Two things materially improve your odds.

First, be logged in with your payment method saved and the exact campground page open before 10:00. Second, know your second and third choice sites by number before you start, because you will not have time to browse.

Several parks hold a portion of sites as first come, first served. These are genuinely obtainable if you arrive at the campground entrance between 8:00 and 10:00 AM on a weekday, and essentially impossible on a Friday in July. Park websites list which loops are held back.

If the park campground is gone, look at national forest campgrounds immediately outside the boundary. They cost less, book far less aggressively, and are frequently a fifteen minute drive from the same trailhead.

Wilderness and backcountry permits

Any overnight trip into the backcountry needs a wilderness permit at nearly every national park. Some are walk-up at a ranger station, some are quota-controlled lotteries months ahead. The famous ones, John Muir Trail exits from Yosemite, Rim to Rim overnights in the Grand Canyon, the Enchantments in Washington, are lotteries with low odds.

Two things people get wrong here. Wilderness permits usually include an in-park entry exemption, which is one of the cleanest ways around a timed entry requirement at Yosemite. And a wilderness permit is not a campground reservation; you will still need somewhere to sleep the night before your hike starts.

Five ways in without a reservation

  • Arrive before the window opens. At most timed-entry parks, entering before 7:00 AM removes the requirement entirely. It is also when the light and the wildlife are best.
  • Arrive after it closes. Entering after 4:00 PM works at Arches and several others. In summer you still get five hours of usable light.
  • Hold an in-park service reservation. Lodging, a campground, or a commercial tour inside the park usually exempts you from timed entry. Check the park page for what counts.
  • Take the next-day release. The 7:00 PM Mountain block the evening before is how a large share of visitors get in.
  • Go midweek in shoulder season. Most timed entry programs end in mid October and do not restart until late spring. A Tuesday in early October at Arches needs nothing at all.

The passes, and which one you need

Entrance fees run $20 to $35 per vehicle for seven days at most parks. The America the Beautiful annual pass is $80 and covers entry to every national park and federal recreation site for a year, for everyone in one vehicle. If your trip includes three or more fee parks, buy it at the first gate.

Several passes are free or discounted and are routinely missed. Active duty US military and their dependents, Gold Star families, and fourth graders with their families all qualify for a free annual pass. There is a lifetime senior pass for US citizens 62 and over, and a free lifetime access pass for people with permanent disabilities.

Note that a pass covers entry only. It does not cover timed entry reservations, lottery permits, campgrounds, or the separate fees charged by state parks like Dead Horse Point in Utah, which sits right in the middle of a lot of Moab itineraries and catches people out every year.

Permit systems, dates and fees change every season and some are announced only weeks ahead. Verify everything on nps.gov and recreation.gov for the specific park before you travel. Nothing on this page is an official source. See our disclaimer.
