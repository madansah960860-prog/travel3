Your first international trip: passports, TSA PreCheck, Global Entry and the 6-month rule

By Ray Okonkwo, Trip planner Published 01/27/2026 Reviewed 01/27/2026 13 min read Category: First timers

Nothing on this list is difficult. All of it is time-sensitive, and the order matters more than the effort. People miss trips over the passport, not over the packing.

Start with the passport, six months before anything else

If you do not have a US passport, apply before you book anything. Routine processing has run roughly four to six weeks in recent years, expedited about two to three weeks, and both figures move with demand. Spring is the worst season because everyone applies at once.

The application itself is straightforward. Form DS-11 for a first passport, done in person at an acceptance facility, which is usually a post office or a county clerk. Bring proof of citizenship (a certified birth certificate, not a photocopy), a government photo ID, a photocopy of that ID, and a 2 inch by 2 inch photo. Fees as of early 2026 are $130 for the book plus a $35 execution fee, with expedited service adding $60.

Two practical notes. First, get the book, not the card. The passport card only works at land and sea borders with Canada, Mexico, Bermuda and the Caribbean; it is useless for any flight. Second, if you have a passport already, check the expiration date today rather than the week before you fly. That leads directly to the next section.

The six-month validity rule, explained properly

Many countries require your passport to be valid for at least six months beyond your date of entry or your date of departure. This is a rule enforced by the destination country, and airlines enforce it at check-in on that country's behalf, because the airline is the party fined if they carry someone who gets refused entry.

What this means in practice: a passport expiring 08/15/2026 may not get you onto a flight to Japan departing 04/01/2026, even though the passport is technically still valid on the day you travel. The airline counts forward six months from arrival and refuses boarding.

The rule is not universal. Most of the European Schengen Area requires three months beyond your intended departure. Canada requires validity only for the length of stay. Japan, Thailand, Egypt, Indonesia, Turkey and many others use the full six months. Because it varies, the only safe habit is to renew whenever your passport is inside nine months of expiring and you have any international travel in mind.

Check the specific requirement on the State Department country information page for your destination. It is the authoritative source and it is updated when rules change.

PreCheck, Global Entry, CLEAR: which one and why

These are three different products and people routinely buy the wrong one.

The simple answer for most first-time international travelers: apply for Global Entry. It includes PreCheck, and the interview requirement is now usually satisfied by Enrollment on Arrival, where a CBP officer interviews you at the airport when you land from your first trip. Conditional approval can take several months, so apply well ahead. Several travel credit cards reimburse the fee.

Do not buy CLEAR expecting it to replace PreCheck. CLEAR moves you to the front of the identity check; the screening lane you enter afterwards is whichever one you are entitled to.

Entry requirements beyond the passport

US passport holders enter most popular destinations without a visa for short stays, but several now require an online registration before departure. These are quick and cheap, and they are enforced at check-in.

  • Japan: Visit Japan Web. Free, ten minutes, turns a 70 minute immigration queue into about 15.
  • Europe: the ETIAS authorization for the Schengen Area has been announced with a phased rollout. Check the official EU page for the current status before you book, because the start date has moved more than once.
  • United Kingdom: an Electronic Travel Authorisation is now required for US citizens. Apply online, small fee, valid multiple entries.
  • Canada: no eTA needed if you fly on a US passport, but you do need one if you hold another nationality's passport.
  • Iceland, Portugal, Italy, Norway: no visa for stays under 90 days in any 180 day period across the whole Schengen Area, which is a combined limit, not per country.

Whatever the destination, check the State Department country page and the destination's own immigration site within a month of departure. Requirements change and the airline will go by the current rule, not the one you read last year.

Put these on the plan, not in your head

Every deadline on this page is a stop with a date. Add them to the trip planner as notes on day one and they print on your itinerary. Passport renewal, Global Entry interview, ETA application, insurance purchase.

Money: cards, cash and the fee that gets everyone

The fee that gets everyone is dynamic currency conversion. A card terminal or ATM abroad asks whether you want to be charged in US dollars or in the local currency. Always choose the local currency. Choosing dollars lets the merchant's bank set the exchange rate, and it is reliably three to seven percent worse than your own bank's rate.

Beyond that, three rules cover almost everything:

  • Carry a card with no foreign transaction fee. The standard fee is three percent, which on a $3,000 trip is $90 for nothing.
  • Use bank ATMs, not the standalone machines in tourist areas, which charge much higher fees and push dynamic currency conversion aggressively.
  • Carry a modest amount of local cash for markets, small restaurants and transit. In Japan, about $80 equivalent. In Iceland, none at all; cards work everywhere including gas pumps and public restrooms.

Tell your bank you are traveling if it still requires that, and carry a second card from a different network stored separately from the first. Card fraud locks are the most common reason travelers end up stranded without access to money.

Your phone abroad

Three options, in ascending order of effort and descending order of cost.

Your carrier's day pass. Roughly $10 to $12 a day on the major US carriers. Zero setup, works the moment you land, and it costs $84 on a seven day trip. Fine for a short trip, expensive for a long one.

An eSIM. Ten to twenty dollars for a week of data in most countries, bought and installed from an app before you leave. Your US number still receives calls and texts over wifi if you enable wifi calling. This is what most of our team uses.

Airplane mode and wifi only. Free, and genuinely workable in cities with good cafe and hotel wifi. It is a bad idea anywhere you are driving, hiking or relying on maps.

Whichever you pick, download offline maps for your destination before you leave. Google Maps offline areas work well and take about two minutes.

Travel insurance, honestly

Two different things get sold under one name. Trip cancellation coverage reimburses prepaid, nonrefundable costs when a covered reason stops you traveling. Travel medical coverage pays for treatment and, critically, medical evacuation while you are abroad.

Most US health plans provide little or no coverage outside the country, and Medicare provides essentially none. Medical evacuation from a remote area can run into six figures. For a trip with hiking, driving in unfamiliar conditions, or any activity where getting hurt means a helicopter, travel medical coverage is the part worth buying.

Trip cancellation is a financial decision. If losing the prepaid cost would genuinely hurt, buy it. If you have booked refundable lodging and a flight with a flexible fare, you have already self-insured most of the risk.

The week-before checklist

  • Photograph your passport page and store it somewhere reachable without your phone.
  • Enroll in the State Department STEP program. It is free and it is how a US embassy reaches you in an emergency.
  • Check in online 24 hours ahead and confirm the airline has your passport number on file.
  • Download offline maps, your boarding pass, and any entry registration confirmation.
  • Confirm your first night's lodging address is written down, not just in an email.
  • Check the plug type and pack the right adapter. Type F for Iceland and most of Europe, Type A for Japan, Type G for the UK.
  • Leave a copy of your itinerary with somebody at home. Our printable checklists include a page for this.

None of this is hard. All of it is easier eight weeks out than eight days out, which is really the entire point of the guide.
