Kyoto

Six to nine days in Japan with Kyoto as the base. Temples at dawn, trains that keep their word, and the best food per dollar we plan.

Copy the 6-day itinerary Build your own
N 35.0116 / E 135.7681 · SEC 01 · OVERVIEW

Overview

Kyoto is 1,600 temples and shrines inside a city the size of Indianapolis, and it rewards early mornings more than any destination we plan.

The city was Japan's capital for a thousand years and was deliberately spared the worst of the 1945 bombing campaign, which is why the wooden architecture survived. It sits in a bowl ringed by mountains on three sides. That geography is why the summers are so humid and why the autumn colour is so good.

For a US traveler, the honest pitch is this: Japan is the easiest country in the world to travel badly prepared. The trains run on time to the minute, the convenience stores sell excellent food at 2:00 AM, crime is close to nonexistent, and staff will walk you to where you are going rather than point. What it demands in return is that you follow the local rules about noise, queueing, and photography, especially in Gion where residents deal with a lot.

The right shape for a first trip is six to nine days with Kyoto as the base and day trips to Nara and Osaka, or a Tokyo entry and Kyoto exit using the Shinkansen between them. Do not try to add Hiroshima to a six-day trip. It is doable and it makes the whole week feel like a train station.

The luggage forwarding trick

Takkyubin services move a suitcase between hotels overnight for about $14. If you are doing Tokyo to Kyoto, send the big bag ahead and ride the Shinkansen with a day pack. It is the single best $14 you will spend in Japan, and it means you never wrestle luggage up a station staircase.

Three day sample plan

See the full 6-day version

Built around one rule: be at the famous thing before 8:00 AM, then spend the afternoon somewhere nobody has heard of. Every stop below is reachable by bus, subway or JR train.

01 Higashiyama, south to north Kyoto core · Day 1 of 3
  • 6:30 AM Kiyomizu-dera at opening

    Opens at 6:00 AM, which is the single best scheduling fact in Kyoto. By 9:30 the approach street is shoulder to shoulder. At 6:30 it is you and a groundskeeper.

    Temple · 1h Travel 18 min by bus $3 entry
  • 8:00 AM Sannenzaka and Ninenzaka lanes

    The stone stepped streets below the temple. Shops open around 10:00, so this hour is for walking, not buying.

    Walk · 45m Travel 5 min on foot $0
  • 9:30 AM Kodai-ji and the Ryozen path

    A quieter temple with a bamboo grove nobody photographs. Ten minutes from the crowds and functionally empty.

    Temple · 1h Travel 12 min on foot $4 entry
  • 12:00 PM Lunch at Nishiki Market

    Standing food, one skewer at a time, down a 400 meter covered arcade. Cash still helps at the smaller stalls.

    Food Travel 22 min by bus $14 per person
  • 3:00 PM Ginkaku-ji and the Philosopher's Path

    Walk the canal path south to north in reverse, ending at the Silver Pavilion. Two miles, flat, shaded, and lined with cherry trees.

    Walk · 2h Travel 25 min by bus $4 entry
  • 6:30 PM Pontocho alley dinner

    One lane wide, running along the Kamo River. Many places have an English menu now; most still prefer a reservation.

    Food Travel 20 min by bus $38 per person
Estimated daily cost $63 per person plus lodging

Bus day pass at $5 covers all transit above. Lodging figured separately at $95 per person for a mid-range hotel double.

02 Arashiyama and the west Kyoto core · Day 2 of 3
  • 7:15 AM Arashiyama Bamboo Grove

    The grove is thirty minutes of walking and it is the most photographed 500 meters in Japan. Before 8:00 AM it is genuinely quiet.

    Sightseeing · 45m Travel 35 min by JR $3 train
  • 8:15 AM Tenryu-ji garden

    Opens 8:30. The garden is 700 years old and was designed to borrow the mountain behind it. Worth the entry more than the grove.

    Temple · 1h Travel 4 min on foot $6 entry
  • 10:30 AM Sagano Romantic Train or a river walk

    The scenic train runs March to December and sells out. If you miss it, the riverside walk north to the monkey park bridge is free and nearly as good.

    Activity · 1h 15m Travel 10 min on foot $6 train
  • 1:30 PM Kinkaku-ji, the Golden Pavilion

    Twenty-five minutes end to end. There is one viewpoint, everybody uses it, and it is still worth going.

    Temple · 45m Travel 30 min by bus $4 entry
  • 4:00 PM Nijo Castle

    Nightingale floors that squeak by design as an intruder alarm. Last entry is 4:00 PM sharp and they mean it.

    Castle · 1h 15m Travel 25 min by bus $9 entry
  • 7:00 PM Izakaya dinner near Karasuma

    Small plates, cheaper than the tourist districts, and the neighborhood where people who work in Kyoto actually eat.

    Food Travel 15 min by subway $31 per person
Estimated daily cost $64 per person plus lodging

A long day with a lot of bus time. If you are tired, drop Nijo Castle and go back to the hotel.

03 Fushimi Inari and a day trip Kyoto core · Day 3 of 3
  • 6:00 AM Fushimi Inari Taisha

    Open 24 hours, free, and completely different before 7:00 AM. Climb past the first two gate tunnels and 90 percent of people turn back.

    Shrine · 2h Travel 12 min by JR $3 train
  • 9:00 AM Breakfast at Inari station

    Convenience store coffee and an onigiri on the platform is not a compromise in Japan. It is genuinely good.

    Food Travel 8 min on foot $6 per person
  • 10:30 AM Nara day trip, deer park and Todai-ji

    45 minutes by train. Todai-ji holds the largest bronze Buddha in Japan inside one of the largest wooden buildings on earth.

    Day trip · 4h Travel 45 min by JR $18 round trip
  • 3:30 PM Nara-machi old town

    Merchant houses, small workshops, and about a third of the visitors that the deer park gets.

    Walk · 1h Travel 15 min on foot $0
  • 6:45 PM Back to Kyoto, dinner at Kyoto Station

    The station building is eleven floors of restaurants and an architectural landmark in its own right. Ramen street is on the tenth.

    Food Travel 50 min by JR $22 per person
Estimated daily cost $57 per person plus lodging

The cheapest of the three days and the one people rate highest afterwards.

Getting around

Do not rent a car. Kyoto is buses, two subway lines, JR trains and a lot of walking. Parking is scarce and expensive and the bus network reaches everything.

Get an IC card, either ICOCA or Suica, at the airport or any station. Tap on, tap off, works on buses, subways, JR trains and most convenience stores across the country. Load about $60 to start.

On the Japan Rail Pass: since the 2023 price increase it no longer pays for a Kyoto-based trip. A 7-day pass costs roughly $340. A Tokyo to Kyoto round trip on the Shinkansen is about $190. Buy individual tickets unless you are covering four or more long legs.

Kyoto buses use a flat fare of about $1.60 inside the city. You board at the back and pay at the front when you get off. A one-day bus pass is $5 and pays off at four rides.

Expect to walk 7 to 10 miles a day. Bring the shoes you already trust, not the new ones.

Etiquette that matters

  • No eating while walking. Stand near the stall and finish there.
  • Phone on silent on all trains. No calls, ever.
  • Private lanes in Gion are marked with no-photography signs and carry a real fine.
  • Tipping is not practiced and can cause confusion. Do not do it.

Where to stay

Pick the neighborhood by what time you want to be moving. Kyoto Station is convenient and charmless. Gion is beautiful and expensive. Downtown is the compromise most people should take.

Safety notes

Japan has one of the lowest crime rates of any country we plan trips to. The real risks here are heat, earthquakes and getting separated from your documents.

  • Summer heat. July and August regularly hit 95 °F with 80 percent humidity. Heat stroke sends visitors to hospital every year. Carry water, take the 1:00 to 4:00 PM block indoors, and use the free cooling shelters marked at station entrances.
  • Earthquakes. Minor tremors are routine. Enable the Japan Meteorological Agency alerts in the NHK World app. In a shake, get under a table, stay away from windows, and wait.
  • Typhoons. August through October. Trains suspend service on announced schedules, which means you get warning if you check the day before.
  • Passport rule. Foreign visitors are legally required to carry their passport at all times. A copy is not sufficient if police ask.
  • Cash. Some small restaurants and shrines are still cash only. 7-Eleven ATMs accept US cards reliably. Bank ATMs often do not.

Numbers to save before you fly

  • Police: 110
  • Fire and ambulance: 119
  • Japan Visitor Hotline, 24 hours, English: 050-3816-2787
  • US Consulate Osaka-Kobe: +81 6 6315 5900

Register the trip with the State Department STEP program. Also photograph your passport page and store it somewhere you can reach without your phone.
