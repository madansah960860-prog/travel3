The PTO math: how to get 17 days off using 9 vacation days

By Ines Ferraro, Head of trip planning Published 02/18/2026 Reviewed 02/18/2026 11 min read Category: Planning

N 45.5152 / W 122.6784 · SEC 01 · BODY

The average full-time American worker with five years of service gets about 15 paid vacation days a year. Roughly half of us do not use all of them. That is not a moral failing, it is a scheduling problem, and scheduling problems have solutions.

The problem is not how many days you have

Most people plan a trip by picking a destination and then asking how many days it needs. That is backwards for anyone working a standard US calendar. Start from the calendar and the destination list narrows itself.

Here is the arithmetic that matters. A week off, Monday through Friday, costs five PTO days and gives you nine days of freedom, counting the weekends on both ends. That is already a 1.8x return. Add one federal holiday and the same five days become ten. Stack two weekends, two holidays and nine PTO days in the right month and you get seventeen consecutive days away from work.

Seventeen days is enough for Japan, Peru, New Zealand or a proper western national parks loop. Nine days is enough for Iceland, Portugal or Utah. The difference between those two lists is not money. It is where you put the days.

The three levers

Lever one: bracket the weekends. Any PTO block should start on a Monday and end on a Friday. Taking Wednesday through Tuesday off costs the same five days and gives you the same nine calendar days, but you burn one weekend sitting at home before you leave.

Lever two: attach a federal holiday. Ten federal holidays exist and most private employers observe six to eight of them. Each one you attach to a PTO block is a free day. Attach two and you have effectively bought back 40 percent of a work week.

Lever three: fly on the edges. A Friday evening departure and a Sunday night return add two full days to the trip at a cost of zero PTO. A Friday 6:00 PM flight out of most US hubs to Europe lands Saturday morning. That is a full Saturday on the ground for the price of leaving the office at your normal time.

Used together, these three levers reliably turn a five day request into eleven days and a nine day request into seventeen.

The 2026 federal holiday calendar, ranked

Not all holidays are worth the same. What matters is whether the holiday falls on a Monday or Friday, and whether there is a second holiday nearby to bridge to.

Four worked examples

The 17-day trip for 9 PTO days: Thanksgiving 2026

Leave Saturday 11/21. Take PTO on 11/23, 11/24, 11/25, then Thanksgiving on 11/26 and the day after on 11/27 are usually company holidays or a cheap single PTO day. Then PTO on 11/30 through 12/04. Return Sunday 12/06.

That is nine PTO days if your employer gives you the day after Thanksgiving, ten if it does not. You get 11/21 through 12/06 inclusive, which is sixteen or seventeen days depending on how you count the return. It is enough for Japan end to end, or Peru with the Sacred Valley, or New Zealand's South Island if you accept two travel days on each side.

The 9-day trip for 4 PTO days: Labor Day 2026

Leave Friday evening 09/04. Labor Day Monday 09/07 is free. Take PTO 09/08 through 09/11. Return Sunday 09/13. Four PTO days, ten calendar days. This is the Iceland trip, and September is the best month to do it.

The 5-day trip for 1 PTO day: Juneteenth 2026

Juneteenth falls on Friday 06/19. Take Thursday 06/18 as your single PTO day and you have Thursday through Sunday. Add Monday 06/22 and it is a five day block for two days of PTO. Enough for a national park, a US city, or a Mexican beach.

The 11-day trip for 6 PTO days: Presidents Day 2026

Leave Saturday 02/14. Presidents Day is Monday 02/16. Take PTO 02/17 through 02/20 and 02/23, 02/24. Return Wednesday 02/25. Six PTO days, twelve calendar days, in the cheapest international travel window of the year outside of January.

The red-eye rule

The single most underused lever for US travelers is the Friday evening transatlantic departure. Most East Coast to Europe flights leave between 5:00 PM and 9:00 PM and land between 6:00 AM and 9:00 AM local. You leave work at your normal time on Friday and you are standing in a European city on Saturday morning, having spent zero PTO days on the crossing.

The same works in reverse coming home from Europe, where most US-bound flights depart between 10:00 AM and 2:00 PM and land the same afternoon. A Sunday return puts you home Sunday evening and back at your desk Monday, tired but employed.

The trade is real. You will lose most of the first day to fatigue. Our advice is to schedule nothing on arrival day except a walk, a meal and an early night. Do not book a museum you paid for. Do not rent a car if you can avoid driving. The Iceland itinerary we publish deliberately puts a hot spring soak on arrival day for exactly this reason.

Going west, the math is worse. Flying to Japan or Australia, you lose a calendar day crossing the date line and the jet lag is harder to shake. For those trips, budget two low-effort days on arrival and treat the first as a write-off rather than pretending otherwise.

How to ask for it without annoying anyone

Long blocks get denied more often than short ones, and the reason is usually coverage rather than policy. Three things that materially improve approval odds:

  • Ask early. Requests submitted more than 90 days out get approved at a much higher rate simply because nothing is scheduled yet to conflict with them.
  • Name the coverage. Say who is handling what, in writing, in the request. A manager approving a two week absence is really approving a plan, not a date range.
  • Avoid quarter close and known crunch weeks. The first two weeks of a fiscal quarter are almost always easier to leave than the last two.

If your employer uses a use-it-or-lose-it policy with a December 31 cutoff, plan the November block. It is the last realistic window and it is also the one everyone else waits too long to claim.

What to do with the days you cannot move

Some PTO cannot be stacked. Half days, days that expire, days your team has already blocked. Use those on domestic long weekends where the flight is short and the cost of a wasted travel day is low. Our destinations page has five options in the three to five day range, including New Orleans and Charleston, both of which are genuinely good with three days and neither of which needs a rental car.

The one thing not to do is hold days back for a trip you are not actively planning. Unused vacation does not roll over at most US employers, and a trip without dates on a calendar does not happen. Pick the block, request it, then figure out where you are going. That order works, and the other one mostly does not.
